AI City — MVP Design Proposal: Mars Colony Builder

product proposal

Mission Design Dossier · Rev 2

Two ships landed.
The third had the crew.

A turn-based Mars colony builder. You command construction drones and two nuclear reactors across a square-grid Martian surface, racing a two-year clock to raise a habitat before the next wave of colonists arrives. There is nobody on the surface to help you.

The premise does mechanical work

Three starships departed Earth. Two arrived. The lost ship carried critical components and every human being assigned to the mission. What survived the transit: one hold of automated construction drones, one hold of nuclear reactors.

This is not just backstory — it sets the MVP's shape. The colony is unmanned. There are no residents to feed, no population to grow, no life support to balance. The entire game becomes a construction and logistics race against a deadline, which is a far tighter first build than a full city-sim, and it gives the lost ship a reason to exist beyond flavour.

Consequence

The win condition is habitat readiness, not population. You are building an empty city for people who are already on their way.

The turn is a drone duty cycle

Drones work for 25 hours, then require a full day on the charger. That cycle is the turn — the clock is measured in robot shifts, not calendar days. A Martian sol runs 24 h 39 m, so a drone works fractionally longer than one Mars day and rests for almost exactly one more.

Fig 1 — The turn, drawn to scale against a Martian sol (24 h 39 m).

Two Earth years of mission time therefore buys you 352 turns. That is the entire budget. Every structure you queue spends some of it.

The mechanic this unlocks — needs your confirmation

Drones recharge on colony power. So the reactor budget doesn't just limit what you can run — it limits how many drones can be on shift. Power and labour become the same constraint, and every reactor is implicitly a decision about construction speed. This is the tension the whole MVP rests on, and it falls straight out of the duty-cycle rule.

The loop

Five beats. The player is always trading power against build time against the shrinking clock.

01Survey the surface, choose two landing sitesTerrain generated from a seed; starting inventory set by the surviving holds

One constraint now, five later

The MVP binds on electricity alone. Everything else is deferred — but the resource ledger is built resource-agnostic from day one, so the later constraints arrive as data entries rather than a rewrite.

Structures occupy real shapes

Buildings are not uniform tiles. They range from single squares to larger, irregular footprints — including L-shapes — and every footprint tile must be in bounds, unoccupied and buildable, or the placement is refused outright. A rejected placement never half-writes.

Screen one — surface survey

The opening move. Elevation and deposits are generated from a seed, then the player sets down both surviving hulls. Site choice must be consequential, so sites are scored on terrain buildability, deposit proximity, and the separation between the two hulls.

Fig 2 — Surface survey. Rings mark mineral deposits; the two framed cells are the landed hulls. Flat ground near deposits scores well; hulls landed far apart are penalised.

Screen two — colony operations

The main view, and where the player spends the game. Grid centre, live constraint meters top, build tray right, construction queue bottom. Everything that can bite you — power margin, drones on shift, turns remaining — stays visible at all times.

Fig 3 — Colony operations. Dashed outline = under construction and producing nothing yet. Power and labour meters are deliberately adjacent, because they are the same decision.

Screen three — cycle report

Ending a cycle produces a readable account of what changed, so the player learns the system by watching it respond. Brownouts are stated plainly, with cause and fix — never a bare error.

EventReport line
CompleteHAB-1 finished. Habitat capacity 16 → 24.

Labour6 drones × 25 h = 150 robot-hours applied. 2 drones held offline — insufficient charge capacity.

Design rule

Brownouts must be deterministic and explainable. Which structure idles first is a documented priority order — never whatever the iteration order happened to be.

Win, lose, and the shape of failure

Victory: habitat capacity meets or exceeds the arriving wave when the clock expires. Only completed structures count — a habitat at 9/10 turns houses nobody.

Defeat: the deadline arrives short of capacity. The colonists are already in transit; there is no extension.

The interesting failure isn't running out of turns — it's over-building habitats early, starving the power budget, dropping drones off shift, and watching the build rate collapse with 200 turns left and no way to recover. That death spiral is the game.

What needs your call

Working defaults are in use so nothing is blocked — but these three change the design if you disagree.

  • Unmanned colony. Confirmed reading: no humans until the wave lands, so no population sim in the MVP. Say the word if you intended a surviving skeleton crew with life support.
  • Recharge draws power. Strongly recommended — it fuses the power and labour constraints into one decision and is what makes reactors interesting. Confirm and it goes into the ledger.
  • Deadline framing. Two Earth years is 352 turns. The real Earth–Mars launch window is ~26 months, which would give 377 turns and make the fiction exact. Your call whether realism or the round number wins.
