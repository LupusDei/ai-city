Resource Chain Proposal 2 of 3 · Silica

Sunlight and Silica — the Photovoltaic Chain

You named this one yourself: regolith → silica → solar panels → power. Following that line through the real numbers turns up something better than a new building. It produces the game's first energy return on investment decision — a structure the player must spend energy to obtain, and which then earns that energy back on a clock.

The clock is 41 turns out of 278. That figure is not a balance knob. It falls out of measured Martian insolation on one side and measured silicon refining costs on the other. Nobody chose it, and it happens to be an excellent number: a solar array is obviously correct on turn 20 and obviously a trap on turn 250.

It is also, as it turns out, immune to the tile-size argument — the payback is identical at a 10 m tile and a 5 m tile. That is worth a section of its own further down, because it is the strongest defence this mechanic has.

Scope, stated up front. This is a post-MVP-loop increment. The MVP's job is to make the electricity/labour loop fun. None of this ships before that loop is proven, and nothing here competes with it for the current sprint. This document exists so the loop is built with the right seams in it.

Every other structure in the catalog is bought with drone-turns. This one is bought with kilowatt-hours, and that changes the kind of question the player is answering. A reactor asks "can I afford the mass?" An array asks "do I have enough mission left to get my energy back?" Those are different decisions, and the second one gets sharper as the deadline approaches. That is free difficulty curve, extracted from real physics rather than a difficulty setting.

The chain

One acquisition utility, one assembly line, one structure. Same shape as the other two chain proposals: extraction is cheap, refining is brutal, and the interesting decision lives at the refining step.

Fig. 1 — One Sifter over-feeds one Furnace by roughly 80×. The upper diagram is schematic; the lower strip is linear, at 576 px = 6,000 kg. The collapse is not a balance choice — it is the ratio between the cost of crushing rock and the cost of purifying silicon.

1. Silica Sifter — the acquisition utility

A beneficiation rake: crush, screen, and concentrate silica-rich fines. Footprint 1×1, three turns to build, drawing 8 kW (20% of one reactor) to yield 6 t of SiO₂ concentrate per turn. That works out to 397 kWh ÷ 6,000 kg = 0.066 kWh/kg, which is the right order of magnitude for mechanical crushing and sifting and nothing more.

This is the first structure in the game that requires a specific deposit tile. That matters beyond this proposal: it is the moment the survey screen's deposit-proximity score stops being decoration and starts being a landing-site decision the player can lose by getting wrong.

The gating is honest. Ordinary Mars soil runs about 45 wt% SiO₂ (Curiosity's APXS and CheMin instruments), which is not a deposit — that is just dirt. But genuinely concentrated opaline silica has been measured: Spirit found opaline silica up to roughly 90% SiO₂ near Home Plate in Gusev crater, and hydrothermal silica has been identified at Nili Patera. Concentrated deposits are real, they are localised, and they are worth landing near. A tile-level requirement is the correct model of that fact.

2. Silicon Furnace — the assembly line

Footprint 2×2, nine turns to build, drawing 36 kW — 90% of an entire reactor. Not 90% of your power budget: 90% of one whole 40 kWe fission unit, running flat out, all turn.

The reason is that making solar-grade silicon is one of the most energy-intensive industrial processes our species runs. Carbothermic reduction of silica to metallurgical-grade silicon costs roughly 11–13 kWh/kg on Earth. Purification to solar grade via the Siemens process adds roughly another 45–60 kWh/kg. Call the total ~60 kWh/kg. Stoichiometry sets the feed rate: SiO₂ → Si is 60.08 → 28.09 g/mol, a 46.7% maximum mass yield, so with process losses budget 2.5 kg of SiO₂ per kg of silicon.

Which gives the throughput, and the shock:

1,788 kWh/turn ÷ 60 kWh/kg = 30 kg of silicon per turn, consuming 75 kg of SiO₂.

Nearly a whole reactor. A full turn. Thirty kilograms.

The Sifter over-feeds the Furnace by 80×, so in practice one Sifter running one turn in eighty keeps the Furnace fed forever. Note also that Sifter and Furnace together draw 44 kW = 110% of one reactor — running both at once needs a second reactor online. That is a real siting and sequencing consequence, and it is the kind of pressure that makes a power grid interesting to manage.

3. Photovoltaic Array — the structure

One tile, 25 m² at a 5 m tile edge. Mars's solar constant is 586 W/m². Mean clear-sky equatorial daily insolation is about 2.9 kWh/m² per sol; at 20% module efficiency that is 0.58 kWh/m² per sol. A turn is 2.014 sols:

0.58 × 2.014 = 1.168 kWh/m² per turn × 25 m² = 29.2 kWh per turn per tile — a 0.588 kW average.

The 5 m tile edge is my own derived recommendation and is pending your ratification. Both the per-tile output and the per-tile silicon cost scale with the square of the tile edge, so if you rule for a different grid every number in this section moves by that square — and, as the next section shows, the payback does not move at all.

State the comparison plainly rather than dressing it up: one array tile is 1.5% of one reactor, so it takes 68 tiles to replace a single reactor — 1,700 m² of panel. Solar on Mars is supplementary, not a substitute. That is the honest answer and not a nerf: 586 W/m² is simply what arrives at 1.52 AU, and pretending otherwise would make every downstream power decision fictional.

Build cost comes from module physics. Real wafers run about 160 µm of silicon at 2.33 g/cm³, and with kerf losses that is roughly 0.8 kg of silicon per m² of module — equivalently the familiar 3–4 g per peak watt, since 20% efficiency puts 200 Wp on each m². So each m² of panel embodies 0.8 × 60 = 48 kWh, and a 25 m² tile is 5 kWp costing 20 kg of silicon.

Which changes the character of the Furnace: 30 kg per turn is one and a half array tiles per turn, not a fraction of one. A single Furnace is a viable engine for a real solar farm rather than a bottleneck that takes three turns to emit one panel — worth knowing before anyone proposes a second Furnace to fix a problem that does not exist.

The payback arithmetic

Now put the two halves together, which is the whole point of this document.

20 kg × 60 kWh/kg = 1,200 kWh to make the silicon, plus about 3.3 kWh of sifting for the 50 kg of SiO₂ feed.

1,200 kWh ÷ 29.2 kWh/turn = 41 turns to pay back. Out of 278.

The same 41 shows up from the other direction, which is a useful sanity check. A Furnace running continuously adds (30 kg ÷ 20 kg) × 29.2 = 43.8 kWh/turn of new generating capacity while consuming 1,788 kWh/turn. 1,788 ÷ 43.8 = 41 turns before the arrays it has produced generate as much as it is drinking. Two independent routes, one number — that is the ratio being real rather than tuned.

The consequence the player must be able to see: an array begun after roughly turn 237 never repays its own construction. It is a net energy loss for the rest of the mission. A trap the player cannot see is just a bad rule, so this is a UI requirement, not a flavour note:

Recommendation. The build panel for any structure with a non-trivial buildCost must show payback in turns against turns remaining, side by side, before the player commits. When payback exceeds turns remaining, say so in plain words.

The payback is scale-invariant — and that is the finding

I derived the tile scale properly and it came out at 5 m, not 10 m. Area goes as the square of the edge, so every per-tile quantity in this chain dropped by 4×: output from 117 to 29.2 kWh/turn, silicon cost from 80 to 20 kg, embodied energy from 4,800 to 1,200 kWh, and tiles per reactor from 17 to 68.

The payback did not move. It is 41 turns either way:

This is not luck. Both the silicon cost and the energy yield are proportional to panel area, so when you divide one by the other the area cancels and the tile size falls out of the expression entirely. What remains is three measured quantities:

48 kWh embodied per m² ÷ 1.168 kWh per m² per turn = 41 turns

where 48 = 0.8 kg/m² × 60 kWh/kg — silicon per m² of module, and the cost of refining it — and 1.168 is Martian insolation through a 20% module over 2.014 sols.

So the payback period is a property of silicon refining and Martian sunlight. It is not a property of our grid, our turn length as a design choice, or any number I picked. The central decision in this proposal cannot be broken by a tuning argument about tile size. Halve the tile again to 2.5 m and it is still 41 turns; the turn-237 cutoff (278 − 41) is likewise untouched, and so is the 45-turn figure for an array nobody ever cleans.

Two practical consequences. First, if you ratify a different tile edge, nothing in this proposal needs re-deriving except the per-tile numbers in the catalog table — the mechanic and the UI requirement stand as written. Second, when someone eventually argues that 41 turns is too punishing, the answer is not a multiplier: it is either a better module efficiency or a cheaper purification route, both of which are real engineering claims that can be argued on the merits.

The three unset cells are deliberate. I have no measured basis for the Sifter's and Furnace's bills of materials or the array's build duration, and this project's number discipline says omit rather than invent. The array's build turns are Decision 1 below, and they matter: they shorten the turn-237 deadline by exactly their own length.

Per shared-foundation item 4, all of these become integers in base units when stored — 6,000 kg is 6_000_000 grams and 29.2 kWh is 29_200 watt-hours. That second one is the argument in miniature: the array's output is not a round number of kilowatt-hours, but it is an exact number of watt-hours. The kW and kg figures in this table are for reading, not for the catalog file. All per-tile quantities here assume the 5 m tile edge and scale as its square.

Risk one: dust deposition

Spirit and Opportunity both measured it: roughly 0.2% of array output lost per sol to settling dust. Over a 2.014-sol turn that is ~0.4% per turn. Arrays decay unless someone cleans them, and cleaning spends the exact same drone-hours the player wants for construction.

So solar carries an ongoing labour tax, not just a build cost — which is a much more interesting property than a flat efficiency number. It also lengthens the payback in a way worth quoting: compounding 0.4% per turn against the 1,200 kWh debt, an array that is never cleaned pays back in 45 turns rather than 41, and the last viable start slides from turn 237 to turn 233. Derived from the figures above, not measured — and scale-invariant for the same reason the 41 is.

Recommendation. Cap cumulative soiling loss — ~60% is a reasonable ceiling. An ignored array should degrade badly and stay useful; it must never become a permanent zero. A structure that silently reaches zero output is indistinguishable from a bug, and the player will report it as one.

Risk two: global dust storms

These are real and they are catastrophic. The 2018 planet-encircling storm drove atmospheric optical depth above τ ≈ 10 and cut Opportunity's insolation by more than 99%. It ended the mission. Storms of this class last weeks to months.

Model them as a scheduled event lasting 10–40 turns that cuts array output by 80–95% while leaving reactors completely untouched. Nothing else in the game needs to know about them.

That asymmetry is the strategic payload. Solar is high-yield and risk-exposed; fission is steady and expensive. "How much of your power base do you dare put on sunlight" becomes a genuine decision, and it arrives free from real Martian weather rather than from a designer inventing a downside to balance an upgrade.

Determinism — this one is not negotiable. Storm timing, duration, and severity must be drawn from the same seeded generator as the terrain, so replaying a seed reproduces the same storms. A storm sourced from unseeded randomness would break the golden-trace harness, and it would break it intermittently, which is the worst possible failure mode: the traces would pass locally and fail in CI with no reproduction.

A scope guard that saves real work

Two things fall out of the turn length that will otherwise get built by someone acting reasonably.

No intra-turn day/night modelling is needed. A turn spans 2.014 sols, so every turn contains two complete Martian day/night cycles. Solar output therefore averages out within a single turn: at this granularity the per-turn average is exact, not an approximation. Nobody should build a sub-turn phase system — the turn length makes it pointless, and it would add a second time axis to a simulation that currently has one.

Batteries are not needed yet. Their textbook job is smoothing diurnal cycling, and the previous paragraph deletes that job at the current turn scale. Their real role here would be dust-storm ride-through and brownout headroom — both of which are mechanics that do not exist yet. Per constitution §8, defer batteries until there is a mechanic that demands them. If storms ship and players want a hedge against them, that is the moment batteries have earned their entry, and they will be cheaper to design then because the storm model will already be specified.

The shared foundation — build it once

All three chain proposals need the same five additions. They should be built once, as a shared prerequisite, not three times inside three features. And to be clear: this proposal does not justify them on its own. Per constitution §8, the abstraction is earned by three real callers — which is exactly the situation, so it is earned, but only jointly.

Today StructureTypeSpec in src/sim/catalog.ts carries id, name, footprint, buildTurns, produces, consumes, habitatCapacity. The gaps:

  1. buildCost: ResourceAmounts — a one-time bill of materials. hard requirement for this chain Today consumes is a per-turn operating draw, so there is nowhere to record "this array costs 20 kg of silicon to build." Without it this entire chain reduces to a silicon counter that goes up and can never be spent — which is not a mechanic, it is a scoreboard.
  2. siting.requiresDeposit?: string hard requirement for this chain The Sifter is the first structure that must sit on a specific deposit. Without this field, deposits are decoration and the survey screen's proximity score is a lie told to the player at the one moment they cannot take it back.
  3. storageCapacity: ResourceAmounts — a stockpile cap. Unbounded accumulation removes every logistics decision, and it matters acutely here: an 80×-over-feeding Sifter will pin any silica cap immediately, which is precisely the signal that should teach the player to idle it. Silent overflow discard is a ledger bug that will not surface until balance work, when it will look like a balance problem.
  4. Integer base units — watt-hours and grams. time.ts argues at length that integers are non-negotiable project-wide for determinism; catalog.ts then accepts any finite non-negative number. Float sums are order-dependent, and this chain is where it bites — a payback readout is a division, and margin checks start hinging on 119.99999 versus 120. Cheap now (a validator change), expensive once golden-trace baselines exist.
  5. Brownout priority as a documented total order, over a much larger consumer set than the MVP has, plus one documented rule for partial power. Recommend binary idle: a plant either runs at full rate or not at all. Fractional throughput reintroduces rounding drift and makes the cycle report far harder to explain — "your Furnace made 17.3 kg of silicon because it got 58% of its power" is not a sentence a player can act on.

What this must not break

The chain touches placement, the power ledger, the build queue, and the seeded generator. These are the regression risks and the tests I want to exist before it merges.

Placement and deposits

  • Deposit exhausted mid-build. A Sifter under construction on a deposit that runs out must resolve to one documented state — I recommend the build completes and the structure idles at zero yield, because cancelling a partly-built structure silently destroys drone-turns the player has already spent. Test both the exhaustion-during-build and exhaustion-after-completion paths.
  • Placement rejected off-deposit. Attempting to site a Sifter on a non-deposit tile must fail with a structured error, and must not partially mutate the tile grid.

Costs and stockpiles

  • Silicon stockpile exactly equal to the 20 kg cost. The boundary case. Build must succeed and the stockpile must land at exactly zero — this is the classic > versus >= off-by-one, and it will be found by a player before it is found by a reviewer.
  • Empty stockpile. Build must be refused up front, not started and stalled.
  • One gram short. 19,999 g must refuse. Asserted in base units, which is the whole argument for foundation item 4.
  • Storage overflow. The Sifter filling a silica cap must clamp, and the discarded amount must appear in the cycle report rather than vanishing.
  • Brownout during a partial build. A half-built array must survive a power shortfall and resume, not lose progress.

Storms, soiling, and the readout

  • Storm beginning mid-build. A storm that starts while an array is under construction must not affect construction; it applies to output only, from the turn the array completes. Reactor output must be provably unchanged across the storm window.
  • Soiling cap boundary. An array held at exactly the cumulative cap must produce the capped output and never less, across many turns. Test the turn on which the cap is reached and the turn after.
  • Storm and soiling composed. A storm during a soiled array's operation must not compound into a negative or below-floor value.
  • Payback readout with zero turns remaining. On turn 278 the readout must render without dividing by zero and must clearly say the build cannot repay.
  • Seeded storm reproducibility. Same seed → identical storm schedule. Assert the full schedule, not just the first storm, and assert it survives a save/load round trip.
  • Determinism end to end. Same seed plus same orders → byte-identical ledger, across a mission that includes at least one storm and one soiling cap event.

Decisions I need from you

  1. What is the array's buildTurns? I have no measured basis for it, so I left it unset rather than invent one. Recommendation: match the Sifter at 3 turns — same 1×1 scale of work, reasoning by analogy and saying so. Cost of being wrong: build turns come straight off the turn-237 deadline. Set it to 12 and the last viable start is turn 225, and every payback readout is optimistic by nine turns.

  2. Soiling cap at 60%? Recommendation: yes, 60%, with the array visibly dimming as it approaches. Cost of being wrong: too low and cleaning drones are pointless labour the player correctly ignores; no cap at all and an unattended array asymptotes to zero, which reads as a bug and makes the whole chain feel like a punishment.

  3. How are storm duration and severity drawn? Recommendation: draw the entire storm schedule at world-gen from the terrain seed, so it is fixed and inspectable before turn 1 rather than sampled per-turn. Cost of being wrong: per-turn sampling advances the RNG stream on every tick and couples storm generation to turn order — a refactor that changes tick ordering then invalidates every golden trace, and the failure looks like a determinism bug rather than a design choice.

  4. Binary idle or fractional throughput under brownout? This is foundation item 5, and the Furnace is its hardest case at 90% of a reactor. Recommendation: binary idle. Cost of being wrong: fractional rates give fractional kilograms, which reintroduce float drift in exactly the ledger we are trying to keep integral, and make the cycle report unexplainable.

Working defaults are in use for all four — nothing is blocked. Say the word on any of them and I will change the numbers, not the architecture.

Scope and sequencing

To restate it, because it is the easiest thing to lose: this is a post-MVP-loop increment. The MVP's job is to make the electricity and labour loop fun, and none of this ships before that loop is proven to be fun. Nothing in this document should pull a single engineer off the core loop.

What I am asking for now is narrower: that the MVP's catalog and power ledger be built with buildCost and siting.requiresDeposit in mind, and with integer base units, because those three are far cheaper before the golden-trace baselines exist than after. The Sifter, the Furnace, and the array can wait. The seams cannot.
